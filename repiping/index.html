Whole-House Repiping in Apopka, FL.

Repiping replaces the old water supply pipes in a house with new PEX or copper, ending repeated leaks, rusty water and low pressure. It is usually considered when a house has the repeated leaks, rusty water or low pressure that point to failing pipe.

Repiping is a big decision, so we start with evidence. Repeated pinhole leaks, discolored water, low pressure and visible corrosion are the signs that pipe is failing, and a plumber should look before you commit.

We explain the options and the cost factors plainly in Apopka, FL. If a spot repair solves it, we say so, and if the pipe is at the end of its life, we show you why.

1

Signs your pipes are failing.

One leak does not mean you need a repipe. Several leaks in a short time, brown or orange water when you first turn on a tap, and pressure that drops across the house are stronger signs. Pipe corrodes from the inside, so the wall may look fine while the pipe is nearly closed.

If you see a single leak, leak detection finds the source first, and a burst pipe repair fixes an urgent failure.

2

Pipe materials compared.

Each has trade-offs.

Material.Strengths.Trade-offs.
PEX.Flexible, quick to install, resists corrosion.Not for direct sun, needs proper fittings.
Copper.Long record, durable, heat tolerant.Higher material cost, can corrode in some water.
CPVC.Lower cost, rigid.Can become brittle with age.
Galvanized steel (old).Common in older homes.Corrodes inside, restricts flow, should be replaced.
3

Slab and attic routing.

In many Florida homes, supply lines run through the attic or under the slab. Attic routing lets the plumber replace pipes with little damage to finished rooms. If the lines under the slab have failed, the options include rerouting new pipe through the attic or walls. See slab leak repair for that decision.

4

How a repipe goes.

A typical whole-house repipe follows these steps.

  1. Inspect and plan. The plumber maps the existing lines and chooses the route.
  2. Shut off water. Water is off for part of the day, and the plumber tells you how long.
  3. Install new lines. New pipe goes in through the attic, walls or crawlspace with small access holes.
  4. Pressure test. The system is tested before walls are patched.
  5. Restore and clean up. Access holes are patched and water is turned back on.
5

Permits and inspection.

A repipe is major work, and permits usually apply. Ask the City of Apopka building division what is required for your address, and the plumber can tell you what is typical. Do not skip the permit, because it protects you when you sell the house.

6

What to ask before you sign.

Ask how the plumber plans to route the new lines, how many access holes the job needs and who patches the drywall. Ask what the quote includes for permits and testing, and what the plan is for keeping water available to the house during the work.

Get the answers in writing. A clear plan protects both sides, and a plumber who is confident in the work will be glad to give it.

7

Spot repair versus a full repipe.

A single leak in an otherwise sound system is a repair. A system with repeated leaks in several rooms is a candidate for replacement, because each repair opens a wall and the next failure is coming. We compare the cost of continued repairs with the cost of replacing the lines so the decision rests on numbers.
