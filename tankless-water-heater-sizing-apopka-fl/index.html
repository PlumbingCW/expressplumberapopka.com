Tankless Water Heater Sizing in Apopka, FL: A Homeowner Guide.

Choosing a tankless water heater is mostly an exercise in two numbers: how much hot water you need at once, and how much the heater has to warm the incoming water. Get either wrong and you end up with lukewarm showers or a unit that costs more than it should.

This guide walks through the math in plain terms, using a worked example for a typical Apopka household.

The two numbers that decide size.

Tankless sizing starts with flow rate, measured in gallons per minute (GPM), and temperature rise, which is the difference between the incoming water temperature and the temperature you want at the tap. A heater is rated by how many GPM it can heat at a given rise.

The bigger the rise and the more fixtures you run at once, the bigger the heater must be. A larger unit costs more and, for electric models, can demand a lot from your electrical panel.

1

Typical flow rates for household fixtures.

These are common ranges. Check the labels on your own fixtures.

Fixture.Typical flow (GPM).
Shower.About 2.0 to 2.5.
Kitchen faucet.About 1.5 to 2.2.
Bathroom sink.About 0.5 to 1.5.
Dishwasher.About 1.0 to 2.0.
Clothes washer.About 1.5 to 3.0.
2

Step one: add up what runs together.

Think about your busiest morning. If two people shower while the dishwasher runs, you may need around 2.5 plus 2.5 plus 1.5, or about 6.5 GPM at once. You do not have to size for every fixture in the house, only for the combination you actually use at the same time.

Be honest about peak use. A household of two that never overlaps showers needs a smaller unit than a family of five.

3

Step two: find your temperature rise.

Subtract the incoming water temperature from the temperature you want. Most people set hot water near 120 degrees. If incoming water is 70 degrees, the rise is 50 degrees.

Florida groundwater is warmer than in northern states, which helps, but it varies through the year and by supply. The plumber can measure your actual incoming temperature, and you can check yours with a thermometer at a cold tap after letting it run.

4

A worked example.

Take a household that peaks at 6.5 GPM with a 50 degree rise. The heater must deliver 6.5 GPM at 50 degrees of rise. Compare that to the manufacturer's chart for each model, and choose a unit that meets it with a little room to spare.

If the chart shows a candidate delivering 7 GPM at that rise, it works. If it tops out at 5 GPM, you will notice a drop in temperature when everything runs at once.

5

Gas or electric.

Gas tankless units deliver higher flow and are the common choice for larger homes, but they need a gas line of the right size and proper venting. Electric units are simpler to vent but draw a lot of power, and a whole-house electric model may need a panel upgrade.

Our tankless water heater page explains installation, and the water heater installation page covers the job in more detail.

6

A short sizing checklist.

Use this before you shop.

  1. List your fixtures. Write down every shower, tub, sink and appliance.
  2. Pick your busiest hour. Add up the fixtures that run together.
  3. Measure incoming temperature. Use a thermometer at a cold tap.
  4. Compare charts. Match your GPM and rise to a model's rated output.
  5. Check the infrastructure. Confirm gas line size or panel capacity before you buy.
7

Common sizing mistakes.

Buying by tank size instead of flow rate is the most common error. Another is ignoring the electrical or gas supply, which can add cost after the unit is chosen. A third is sizing for a single fixture only, and then finding the shower runs cold when someone starts the dishwasher.

If you are unsure, have a plumber look at the house. A short visit often saves a return trip.

Sizing for Apopka's conditions.

Apopka's climate is humid subtropical, with a record low of 15 degrees and a record high of 100 degrees, per climate data for the city. In a cold snap incoming water gets colder than usual, so a unit sized with no margin can fall short on the coldest morning.

Hot water scale shortens the life of tankless units. The City of Apopka's utility pages list billing and service information, and your water quality report is the place to learn what is in the supply. Test your tap before you decide whether to add a filter or softener.

Standards and sources. Gas water heater work follows the manufacturer's installation instructions and the National Fuel Gas Code (NFPA 54), and plumbing work in Florida falls under the Florida Building Code, Plumbing. We cite these by name and do not quote section numbers from memory, so ask the City of Apopka building division which requirements apply to your address.
